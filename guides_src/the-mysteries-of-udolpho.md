# A Reader's Guide

*Radcliffe was the most popular and highly paid novelist of her age, and* The Mysteries of Udolpho *its defining book. The questions below are for book clubs, students, and solitary readers who want to sit with the novel a while longer. They are best taken up after finishing the book, as some touch on later events.*

## Ann Radcliffe: A Brief Timeline

- **1764** — Ann Ward born in London, into a comfortable, well-connected family.
- **1787** — Marries William Radcliffe, a journalist and later a magazine editor, whose work leaves her long evenings free to write.
- **1789** — Publishes her first novel, *The Castles of Athlin and Dunbayne*, anonymously.
- **1790–1791** — *A Sicilian Romance* and then *The Romance of the Forest* build her reputation as a mistress of suspense and landscape.
- **1794** — *The Mysteries of Udolpho* appears and becomes the sensation of the decade; her publisher pays a then-unheard-of five hundred pounds for it.
- **1796** — Matthew Gregory Lewis publishes *The Monk*, the horror answer to Radcliffe's terror, and the scandal sets the two writers in permanent contrast.
- **1797** — Radcliffe replies with *The Italian*, often thought her finest and most controlled novel.
- **1790s onward** — Famous and comfortably off, she withdraws from public life almost entirely, travels, keeps journals of scenery, and publishes no more novels in her lifetime.
- **1823** — Dies in London at fifty-eight. An essay of hers on the difference between terror and horror is published after her death, along with a final romance.
- **After 1823** — Her influence runs through Austen's affectionate parody, the Brontës, Poe, and the whole later literature of the Gothic and the sublime.

## Questions for Discussion

1. Radcliffe builds her dread almost entirely by suggestion and delay, and then explains every apparent supernatural event by natural means. Did the explanations satisfy you or disappoint you? What does Radcliffe gain, and what does she risk, by refusing to let a single real ghost walk?

2. St. Aubert warns Emily on his deathbed against an excess of sensibility, against feeling so unchecked that it disables reason. How well does Emily heed him? Where in the novel does her feeling serve her, and where does it endanger her?

3. Montoni is one of the great villains of English fiction. What makes him frightening? Is it his cruelty, his greed, his intelligence, or the sheer force of his will, and how does he differ from a mere monster?

4. The landscape in *Udolpho* is not merely scenery. Choose a passage of description, a storm, a mountain view, the first sight of the castle, and consider how the setting carries the emotion of the scene. How does Radcliffe use the sublime?

5. The black veil is the most famous image in the book, and its secret is withheld for hundreds of pages. How did the long suspense affect your reading? Were you satisfied when the truth was finally revealed?

6. Emily spends much of the novel confined, at Udolpho and elsewhere, and much of her heroism is a matter of endurance, patience, and self-command rather than action. Is she a passive heroine, or is there a kind of strength the book is asking us to recognize?

7. In *Northanger Abbey*, Jane Austen gently mocks the reader who takes *Udolpho* too literally and sees Gothic plots in ordinary life. Having read the novel, do you find Austen's joke fair? What is the difference between being moved by a book and being misled by it?

8. Radcliffe stops the narrative to give Emily poems of her own. Read one of them aloud. What does the verse add to your sense of Emily's mind, and how does it change the pace of the chapter around it?

9. Critics contrast Radcliffe's "terror," which suggests and withholds, with the "horror" of Lewis's *The Monk*, which shows the dreadful thing directly. Which do you find more powerful, and why? Can a story frighten you more by what it hides than by what it reveals?

10. *Udolpho* is a long book that asks for patience, and its first readers gave it that patience gladly, waiting for each volume. What did the length and the slow withholding do for you as a reader? What would be lost if the same story were told at half the length?
