# A Reader's Guide

*Lewis wrote* The Monk *at nineteen, in a matter of weeks, and it made him famous and infamous at once. The questions below are for book clubs, students, and solitary readers who want to sit with the novel a while longer. They are best taken up after finishing the book, as some touch on later events.*

## Matthew Gregory Lewis: A Brief Timeline

- **1775** — Matthew Gregory Lewis born in London, son of a wealthy family with plantation holdings in Jamaica.
- **1791–1792** — Educated at Westminster School and Oxford; travels in Germany and immerses himself in German literature, then almost unknown in England.
- **1794** — Serves as an attaché at the British embassy at The Hague; at nineteen, drafts *The Monk*, by his own account, in roughly ten weeks.
- **1796** — *The Monk: A Romance* published in London. Lewis, elected to Parliament for Hindon, adds his name and "M.P." to the second edition; scandal follows.
- **1797** — Coleridge reviews the novel, praising its genius and condemning its content; Ann Radcliffe answers it with *The Italian*.
- **1798** — Under threat of prosecution, Lewis issues a softened fourth edition. He is "Monk" Lewis for the rest of his life.
- **1790s–1810s** — A successful dramatist and poet; his Gothic drama *The Castle Spectre* (1797) is a hit, and his ballads circulate widely.
- **1815–1818** — Inherits Jamaican estates; twice sails to the Caribbean and keeps a journal of what he sees of slavery on his own plantations.
- **1818** — Dies of yellow fever at sea, returning from Jamaica, at the age of forty-two.

## Questions for Discussion

1. Ambrosio is introduced as a man universally admired for a virtue that has never been tested. How much of his fall is caused by temptation from outside, and how much by the pride and inexperience that his sheltered "virtue" has bred in him?

2. Lewis sets Ambrosio's story against the love of Raymond and Agnes, and against the cruelty of the Prioress of St. Clare. What does the novel gain by braiding these plots together? What themes pass back and forth between them?

3. Critics distinguish Lewis's "horror," which shows the dreadful thing directly, from Ann Radcliffe's "terror," which suggests and withholds. Where in the novel did you feel Lewis choosing to show rather than suggest, and what was the effect on you as a reader?

4. The novel is fiercely critical of the monastery and the convent as institutions. Is its target specifically Catholic religion, or something broader about any system that demands the total suppression of desire? How do you read Elvira's more private, domestic faith by contrast?

5. Women in the novel are repeatedly confined, whether in convents, in the family home, or in the tomb. Consider Antonia, Agnes, and Matilda. How does each respond to confinement, and what does the book seem to be saying about the control of women?

6. Matilda is one of the most debated figures in Gothic fiction. Trace how your understanding of her changes across the novel. Does the final revelation about her deepen the story or cheapen it?

7. The supernatural in *The Monk* is real: the ghost, the demon, and the pact all genuinely exist within the story. How would the novel change if Lewis had explained these away, as Radcliffe habitually did?

8. Lewis stops the narrative to insert full ballads such as "The Water-King" and "Alonzo the Brave and the Fair Imogine." What do these poems add? Try reading one aloud. How does it change the pace and mood of the chapter around it?

9. The book was written by a twenty-year-old and is preoccupied with youth, inexperience, and the disasters of first desire. Where do you feel the author's youth in the writing, for good or ill?

10. *The Monk* was nearly prosecuted, then bowdlerized by its own author, and has never quite left the edge of respectability since. What in it still has the power to shock, and what once shocked readers but no longer does? What does that difference reveal about how our sense of the forbidden has changed?
