# A Reader's Guide

## Questions for reflection and discussion
1. *A Honeymoon in Space* was written before the airplane, the rocket, or any true knowledge of the other planets. How does reading it as a "time capsule" of 1901 change your experience of it? What did the book make you realize about how confidently each age pictures the future?
2. Griffith frames a tour of the solar system as a honeymoon. Why do you think he chose a newly married couple as his travelers, and what does the romance add to, or take away from, the adventure?
3. Zaidie Rennick is quick, brave, and outspoken, unusually so for a heroine of 1901. How modern did she feel to you? Where does the novel still show the assumptions of its era about women, class, or empire?
4. Each world the *Astronef* visits, the dead Moon, the canal-laced Mars, the green young Venus, is imagined according to the best science of Griffith's day. Which of his guesses surprised you as being close to the truth, and which now read as charmingly wrong?
5. The travelers repeatedly meet civilizations either far above humanity or fallen into ruin. What do you think Griffith is saying, through these encounters, about the direction of his own confident, industrial, imperial age?
6. Griffith was once the best-selling scientific-romance writer in Britain, then was eclipsed by H. G. Wells and largely forgotten. Having read this, how would you describe what he does well, and why do you think Wells endured while Griffith did not?
7. The "R. Force" antigravity and the "luminiferous ether" are invented or obsolete science, yet the book asks you to accept them completely. Did the imaginary physics get in your way, or did you find it easy to surrender to? What makes a piece of invented science convincing?
8. Compare the mood of this book with modern science fiction you know. What has been gained, and what has been lost, between Griffith's wonder-struck grand tour and today's darker or more technical visions of space?
9. The novel began as separate magazine installments, one planet at a time. Can you feel that episodic origin in the finished book? Did it help or hinder the story as a whole?
10. If you could hand *A Honeymoon in Space* to a reader who had never heard of George Griffith, how would you sell it to them in a sentence?

## George Griffith: a brief timeline
- **1857** Born George Chetwynd Griffith-Jones in Plymouth, Devon, son of a clergyman; largely home-schooled from his father's library.
- **1870s–1880s** Runs away to sea as a young man; works as a traveler, teacher, and journalist.
- **1893** *The Angel of the Revolution* is serialized in *Pearson's Weekly* and becomes the first best-selling "scientific romance," making Griffith the leading writer of the future-adventure in Britain.
- **1894** Publishes the sequel *Olga Romanoff*; formally adopts the byline "George Griffith."
- **1895–1898** H. G. Wells emerges with *The Time Machine* (1895) and *The War of the Worlds* (1898); the publisher Pearson increasingly turns to Wells.
- **1900** "Stories of Other Worlds," the interplanetary serial that will become *A Honeymoon in Space*, runs in *Pearson's Magazine*.
- **1901** *A Honeymoon in Space* is published in book form, the same year as Wells's *The First Men in the Moon*.
- **1904–1906** With sales in decline, moves his family to Port Erin on the Isle of Man for the lower cost of living.
- **1906** Dies at Port Erin, aged 48, his pioneering role in science fiction already fading from public memory.

## If you loved A Honeymoon in Space
Seek out Griffith's earlier best-sellers, *The Angel of the Revolution* (1893) and *Olga Romanoff* (1894), the future-war epics that made his name, and set him beside his great rival H. G. Wells, especially *The First Men in the Moon* (1901), for a fascinating side-by-side of how two writers imagined the same year's future.
