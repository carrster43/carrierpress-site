Who represents you, with a source on every line

Welcome back. The biggest thing on the site this week is free, has no account, and is not a book.

This week: who represents you

There are now fifty-six new pages at Who represents you, one for every state and territory. Each one names the people holding office there right now: the governor and the statewide offices, the seats that state sends to the US Senate and the US House with the Washington address and line for each, and a count of the seats in its own legislature.

What makes them worth your time is the line under every name. It says where that name came from and the date it was last checked. The governor of Mississippi is sourced to the governor's own office; the auditor to the auditor's. Federal rows come from the Senate and from the Clerk of the House and refresh every week. This week every statewide row was put through that check again against the office's own website, and each one now carries the date it was last confirmed.

That is an unusual thing to publish. Most pages like these tell you a name and expect you to take it on faith, and they are quietly wrong for months after an election. These say where they looked and when, so you can judge how much to trust a line, and go to the source if you want to check it yourself.

The state legislators are deliberately not listed. A state has dozens or hundreds of them and two or three are yours, and that is a question about your address rather than your state. Who Serves answers that one: one address in, and back come only the districts and offices that cover it, the state legislative seats included, with when each is next up for election.

One to read

All About Feelings by Jeffrey L. Carrier. There is a shelf on the site called Money Skills and Making Things, full of books about side hustles, startups and building things online for readers starting early, and sitting among them is a coloring book about feelings. It belongs there. Before a child can plan a lemonade stand or stay with a project that is not working yet, they need a name for frustrated, and bored, and brave. Most grown-ups remember being told to calm down. Fewer remember being asked what the feeling was called. There are fourteen feelings in this one, each with a page to colour and a prompt about when that feeling turns up, written for ages 5 to 8. If you have a child at the table this weekend, grateful is a good page to start on. It sits right beside jealous and bored, which is about how it goes in real life too.
