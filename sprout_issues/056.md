# This week: a song that keeps a record

Some of what a family passes down is never written. A song learned at a kitchen table travels
with the people who sing it, across seas and generations, and nobody thinks to ask what it is
counting.

[The Sixth Cache](/books/the-sixth-cache/) is built on exactly that. In Sfax, Sami Kouris
records his grandmother singing the song her mother taught her. He has heard it eight times.
The ninth time, listening back through headphones, he counts the saints in her verse and
finds they fall in an order no Orthodox calendar has ever kept. The song, carried from
Kalymnos to Tunisia a century ago, turns out to be a cipher, and the cipher leads through a
Marseille warehouse archive to an unrecorded cache on a Kerkennah island.

The Things Left Open novels keep finding records like this, hidden in plain sight. In
[The Mastic Codex](/books/the-mastic-codex/), an archivist in Tarpon Springs is cataloguing a
century of Greek import papers when she finds receipts for mastic harvests in February. Mastic
is bled from the trees in summer. The impossible dates are not errors. They cluster, they
count, and they point across the ocean to a manuscript sealed for four centuries in a mountain
monastery on Chios.

In both books the hard part comes after the code is broken: deciding who the truth belongs to.

# One to read

**[The Sixth Cache](/books/the-sixth-cache/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. Sami follows the cipher
and finds his own family named in the record, not as one of the network's protected lines but
as its watchers: trusted to see the worst and never to speak. A disinherited rival wants the
secret exposed for profit, the network's last witnesses want it contained, and Sami wants it to
answer for itself. It is Book 3 of Things Left Open and stands on its own, a novel about what a
family owes the truths it was trusted to keep silent. Read it while the old songs are out, and
it is available on Amazon.
