# This week: what the island kept in plain sight

The Marjorie Corey Files are set on Patchogu Island, six miles of sand and sea grass off
the North Carolina Outer Banks. Three friends inherit the Ginger Mae Coffee Shop there, and
with it sixty years of what the town agreed not to say out loud. What makes the series
unusual is where the secrets are kept. Not in a vault, mostly. On the walls and the
shelves, where everyone has walked past them for years.

In [The Recipe Notebooks](/books/the-recipe-notebooks/), Grandma Knapp's old notebooks
sit under glass behind the counter as a memorial, until a visiting scientist stops cold at
the display, photographs three pages and hurries out. Three of the notebooks turn out to be
written in her hand but in no recipe anyone could cook. The quantities are in milligrams.

In [The Prattler Files](/books/the-prattler-files/), the island newspaper has kept track
of every storm, election and shop opening since 1934, except for one gap. Sixteen issues
are missing from the archive. Not lost. Removed.

And in [The Sea Captain's Portrait](/books/the-sea-captain-s-portrait/), the secret has
been hanging in the back hallway the whole time. Each book stands alone, so you can start
with whichever object catches you: a notebook, a newspaper, or a painting.

# From the Labs

- **[Through the Gate](/apps/throughthegate/)** shows the record a resume parser actually
  keeps, and everything it threw away. Paste the text of your resume and you see the fields
  it kept, the job titles it renamed with your own wording beside them, and every piece that
  did not survive, each with the plain reason it was lost. It is not any one employer's
  system and does not predict whether you will be screened in or out; it shows you the
  version of you that gets filed.

# One to read

**[The Sea Captain's Portrait](/books/the-sea-captain-s-portrait/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier.
For years everyone on the island assumed the weathered sea captain in the Ginger Mae's back
hallway was Grandma Knapp's grandfather, until a retired art authenticator asked to examine
it and found an 1847 oil of real museum value. An estate lawyer arrives from the mainland
with a claim and very good paperwork, calls from the ferry landing to say she has found
something in the county records, and never reaches the island. Olivia traces deeds,
ledgers and altered records back a hundred and fifty years, to the free Black merchant
captain who painted himself into the island's history. It is Book 3 of the series and
stands alone, a short mystery about provenance, memory and what a place truly owns, and it
is available on Amazon.
