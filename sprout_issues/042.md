# This week: the chapbook in the peddler's pack

Before there were children's publishers, there were chapbooks: cheap, thin, hand-illustrated popular books that peddlers sold for pennies and children read to pieces. Thousands were printed in the early American republic. Very few survive, because they were made to be used up.

[A New History of Blue Beard](/books/a-new-history-of-blue-beard/) is one of them. It was printed at Sidney's Press in New Haven in 1806, adorned with cuts, and its title page offers it "for the amusement of Little Lack Beard and his pretty sisters." The tale is Charles Perrault's: a nobleman with a blue beard hands his young wife the keys to his house, forbids her one small room, and leaves. But the names are not Perrault's. Abomelique, Fatima, Irene and Selim came from a London stage melodrama of 1798, which is why a nursery book arrives complete with elephants, kettledrums and silken canopies. What American children got was the fairy tale as it came back from the theater.

The same shelf has gentler things to read at bedtime. [The Surprise Book](/books/the-surprise-book/), from 1918, follows Dotty as she collects a story for every month to paste into a scrapbook for Christmas; it was made to be read a chapter at a time. [Artful Anticks](/books/artful-anticks/), gathered in 1894 from The Century Magazine, is Oliver Herford's verse and pictures about small creatures with one entirely human ambition, followed with a straight face until it collapses.

# One to read

**[A New History of Blue Beard](/books/a-new-history-of-blue-beard/)** by Gaffer Black Beard. The most
famous names in this American nursery book were borrowed from a hit London melodrama, not from the
fairy tale. This annotated edition gives the complete 1806 text with its original woodcuts, spelling and
punctuation, an introduction on how the story reached America, notes on the old words, and Perrault's
1697 tale for comparison. It does not soften the forbidden room, so it is best read together, and it
is available on Amazon.
