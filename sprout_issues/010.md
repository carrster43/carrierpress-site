Late January is when the big money resolutions start to wobble. This issue goes looking for
where those resolutions came from in the first place.

# This week: an old argument about money

Most advice about getting on in life has a family tree, and one book on the Classics Line
prints three generations of it in a single volume.

The oldest is from 1758. A Philadelphia printer, Benjamin Franklin, gathered twenty five
years of his own proverbs into one speech and gave it to an invented old man to deliver.
That speech is The Way to Wealth.

The middle one was spoken, not written. In the 1870s a Baptist minister, Russell H. Conwell,
began telling a story about a farmer who sold his land to go hunting for diamonds. He told it
more than six thousand times, and used it to put poor students through the university he
founded. That is Acres of Diamonds.

The youngest is [The Science of Getting Rich](/books/the-science-of-getting-rich/), a short
book from 1910 by a midwestern writer, Wallace D. Wattles, which claims that wealth follows a
law as reliable as gravity. Wattles died the year after it came out and was forgotten for most
of a century, until The Secret named him as its inspiration.

The three are rarely bound together. This edition prints them in reverse order, newest first,
so that reading straight through walks the idea back to its source. It is a good way to spend
a resolution month: not with a new plan, but with a clear look at the old ones, and at where
each argument holds and where it turns.

  with someone patient on the other end. Choose a language at your level and a situation, such
  as a bakery that has run out of what you wanted, and the other person speaks first; you

  understood, and no lessons, no streaks and no points.

# One to read

**[The Science of Getting Rich](/books/the-science-of-getting-rich/)** by Wallace D. Wattles.
The book that The Secret named as its inspiration had been out of sight for most of a century,
and its author died the year after writing it. This edition binds it with Conwell's Acres of
Diamonds, the lecture itself, and Franklin's The Way to Wealth, with an introduction tracing
the line from Franklin to Wattles and naming plainly where Conwell's argument turns, a headnote
for each work and a glossary of the people and places that have gone dark since. Franklin's
old man still has the best line: "Dost thou love life? Then do not squander time, for that is
the stuff life is made of." It is available on Amazon.
