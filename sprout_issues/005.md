# This week: the art of saying less

Most horror tells you too much. James tells you almost nothing, and that is the method.

A scholar opens a book. A clergyman inherits a house. A verger mentions in passing that the
new altar tomb has a crack in it. Nothing is described more than it has to be, and James
never once raises his voice. The reader does the rest, and the reader's imagination is
always worse than anything a writer could put on the page.

The best example may be "Casting the Runes", which the cinema turned into Night of the Demon.
It turns on a man who is handed a slip of paper he never notices taking, and who cannot give
it away. There is barely a monster in it. There is a piece of paper, and a deadline.

Restraint runs right through the older half of the weird fiction shelf. Robert W. Chambers
used the same trick in [The Mystery of Choice](/books/the-mystery-of-choice/): Breton legend,
buried violence, and the aftermath shown instead of the thing itself.

If you read [Ghost Stories of an Antiquary](/books/ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/) last
week, [More Ghost Stories of an Antiquary](/books/more-ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/) is
where James goes next. Between the two volumes you have every story from his first three
books.

# One to read

**[More Ghost Stories of an Antiquary](/books/more-ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/)** by M. R.
James. Casting the Runes, the story behind Night of the Demon, is here, and it remains the
best thing anyone has written about a man who cannot give a piece of paper away. This
volume holds James's second and third collections entire, More Ghost Stories of 1911 and A
Thin Ghost and Others of 1919, twelve stories in his own order, with both of his prefatory
notes that most selected editions drop. A headnote comes before each story, and a glossary
explains the cathedral and antiquarian words. Save it for the longest nights of the year;
it is available on Amazon.
