# This week: money fables from the classics shelf

A story about money is nearly always a story about its own year.
[6,000 Tons of Gold](/books/6000-tons-of-gold/) appeared in 1894, one year after the
Panic of 1893, while America tore itself apart over the gold standard. Henry Richardson
Chamberlain, London correspondent of The New York Sun, answered the quarrel with a thought
experiment: what if one man suddenly owned more gold than most governments on earth?

His hero, Robert Brent, does not hide the fortune or spend it on palaces. He turns it into
a lever. He breaks the speculators of Wall Street, showers mysterious benefactions on the
world, and moves at last to head off a European war. The catch is simple: gold is only valuable because it is scarce, and a man who can end scarcity
cannot do it without breaking something.

Two other books on the same shelf make good company for it. In 1873, four young Americans
nearly walked off with the Bank of England's fortune, and
[The Man Who Robbed the Bank of England](/books/the-man-who-robbed-the-bank-of-england/) is
the confidence man's own story, told by Austin Bidwell.
[The Science of Getting Rich](/books/the-science-of-getting-rich/) by Wallace D. Wattles is
bound as a three in one with Acres of Diamonds and The Way to Wealth: three men, a hundred
and fifty years, one argument.

Read side by side they make an odd and lively set: a fable, a thief's confession and a
long argument about getting on, each written by someone sure he had worked out how money
really behaves.

# One to read

**[6,000 Tons of Gold](/books/6000-tons-of-gold/)** by Henry Richardson Chamberlain. The
author put a footnote on short selling into his adventure story, and this edition keeps it,
which tells you how seriously an 1894 novel took its money. It begins aboard a steamer
bound for Buenos Ayres, where Robert Brent meets a shrewd Scotsman named Duncan Fraser, and
a secret in the Cordilleras becomes a hoard big enough to unsettle every price, wage, debt
and treaty in the world. It is adventure, early scientific romance and financial satire at
once, and it still reads as a sharp fable about abundance promised without cost. This
restored, annotated edition, with an original Preface and a reader's guide, is available
on Amazon.
