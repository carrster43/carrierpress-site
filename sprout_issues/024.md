# This week: space travel before the airplane

[A Honeymoon in Space](/books/a-honeymoon-in-space/) was published in 1901, two years
before the Wright brothers left the ground. Nobody had yet flown a powered aircraft, and
George Griffith sent a pair of newlyweds to the Moon, Mars, Venus and beyond in a private
ship of their own.

That is the pleasure of an old future. You get to see what a generation took for granted
and what it simply could not picture. Griffith's English lord and his American bride
travel in a gravity-defying vessel, and this edition's notes explain the Astronef and the
ether for a modern reader, along with an introduction on what 1901 imagined space travel
would be. Griffith himself is half forgotten now, a rival whose scientific romances H. G.
Wells reportedly envied.

He was not the only writer of his day dreaming at that scale.
[6,000 Tons of Gold](/books/6000-tons-of-gold/), from 1894, is an early scientific romance
of a different kind, asking what would happen if one man suddenly owned more gold than
most governments on earth.

And if you would rather travel by land, [The Mysteries of Udolpho](/books/the-mysteries-of-udolpho/)
by Ann Radcliffe carries orphaned Emily St. Aubert off to a brooding castle in the Apennines.
First published in 1794, it is the founding text of Gothic romance, and the book Jane
Austen's heroine could not put down.

# One to read

**[A Honeymoon in Space](/books/a-honeymoon-in-space/)** by George Griffith. It imagines a
private spaceship touring the solar system at a time when no one had yet flown a powered
airplane, and it does so with real dash and wonder. An English lord and his American bride
discover wonders and dangers on every world they visit, which makes it a gem of
proto-science-fiction and a very good read aloud. This annotated edition adds an original
introduction, notes on the text, discussion questions and a Griffith timeline, and it is
available on Amazon.
