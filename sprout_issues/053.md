# This week: the mistake that passes every check

Here is a strange fact about printed books. A hardcover can be printed with its spine upside
down and pass every check on its way to the shelf. The geometry is exact. Every element
clears every published limit. No automated test would catch it, because an inverted spine
has perfect measurements.

That is the opening of [The Defects That Pass Review](/books/the-defects-that-pass-review/),
and it is the whole argument in miniature. The expensive defects are not the ones that get
rejected. They are the ones that look fine: a margin mirrored the wrong way, the hinge on a
hardcover that a paperback does not have, a spine stretched when it should have been rebuilt.
They pass validation, reach print, and sit quietly while every dashboard reports that all
is well.

You do not have to make books to enjoy the idea. It is the same instinct that runs through
the [Standalone Novels](/fiction/#h-standalone) shelf, whose stories share nothing but the
moment somebody decides not to look away. In [The Last Adviser](/books/the-last-adviser/), the
man who built one of Boston's most trusted wealth firms has six months to choose who runs it
next, and a diagnosis he has told no one. His wife has been keeping a quiet record since the
winter. Somebody, in the end, always does the looking.

# One to read

**[The Defects That Pass Review](/books/the-defects-that-pass-review/)** by Jeffrey L.
Carrier. It records seven measured caliper readings from IngramSpark that contradict every
published spine calculator, and it leaves its own corrections in on purpose, because a rule
that was wrong and later fixed teaches more than one that was right all along. It is a manual
for anyone who prints books, built from measurement: interiors, paperback covers, hardcovers
and verification, with twenty figures drawn to scale and a one-page checklist to use before
any upload. A good book for the end of a year, when it is worth checking the things that have
been quietly fine. It is available on Amazon.
