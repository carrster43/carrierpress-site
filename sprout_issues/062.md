# This week: the first company

Most stories about business are about money. The good ones for young readers are about
something else: what happens to a friendship when it turns into a partnership. The Money
Skills and Making Things shelf is written for readers who are starting early, and three of
its books come at that question from different sides.

In [The Startup Club](/books/the-startup-club/), four high school students meet at the
kickoff for a national youth innovation competition and form a company. Maya writes the
code, Liam runs the strategy, Chloe designs the experience and Noah tells the story. What
nearly ends them is not the well funded rival. It is the equity split.

[The Digital Founders](/books/the-digital-founders/) opens past midnight, with the laptop
fan humming, as Alex writes the first line of code for a company that does not exist yet.
By morning it has a name. From there a group of teenage builders meets the cofounder
argument, the failure with no clean fix, and the place where the fastest path and the
right path stop being the same road.

For the reader who would rather do it than read about it, [Side Hustle
Junior](/books/side-hustle-junior/) is a step by step guide for kids from ten to fourteen.
It opens with Maya again, a different Maya, who drew twelve cats with tiny crowns one
Saturday, packaged them as a sticker set, and found by Sunday night that eleven strangers
had bought it.

Two novels and a handbook, and one lesson under all three: what you build says something
about who you were while you built it.

# From the Labs

- **[Books for One](/apps/soleledger/)** keeps a freelancer's books: income, deductions,
  quarterly estimates and 1099s for one self-employed person. Add a receipt or a 1099 as a
  photo or PDF and it fills in the entry, and enter the estimated tax you plan to pay this
  year and it splits it across the four federal due dates and tracks what you have paid. It
  never applies a tax rule to you, it is not tax advice, and your books stay in the browser
  you use.

# One to read

**[The Startup Club](/books/the-startup-club/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. The hardest moment
in this book does not come from a rival but from a tester, who shows the four founders that
the software they are building underdiagnoses patients with darker skin. That software,
Catalyst, is meant for children whose rare neurological disorders go undiagnosed for
years, and one of those children is Maya's cousin Rosa, who is twelve and has been waiting
for three. It is a smart, fast coming of age novel about ambition, friendship and the
distance between speed and integrity, and a good one to hand a teenager who is about to
start something. It is available on Amazon.
