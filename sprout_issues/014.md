# This week: the ordinary lives beside a first time

Some events arrive as the first of their kind. Afterwards they happen so often that hardly
anyone remembers there was a first. The novels on [The American Firsts](/fiction/#h-american-firsts)
shelf go back to that first time and tell it from inside the ordinary lives that were
standing there when it happened: a farm wife, a sixteen year old runner, a street of shops.

[Mrs. Kehoe](/books/mrs-kehoe/) is set in Bath, Michigan, in the months before May 18,
1927. Almost all of it happens in a farmhouse, at a kitchen table, and every ordinary day
in it reads twice at once: as what it is, and as what you already know it to be.
[One Minute Past Noon](/books/one-minute-past-noon/) starts at a single minute on Wall
Street in September 1920 and follows the six years after it.
[Twelve Minutes on River Road](/books/twelve-minutes-on-river-road/) opens on the 3200
block of River Road in East Camden, a working street with a shoe repair, a barber with a
wooden horse for the children, and a corner drugstore.

Each book stands alone, and each carries an Author's Note that separates the documented
record from invention, so you always know which is which. That note is worth saving for
last. Read after the story has done its work, it shows you how much of what you just felt
was simply true.

# One to read

**[Mrs. Kehoe](/books/mrs-kehoe/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. Nellie Kehoe left seven words
that anybody wrote down, and on the list of the dead attached to the jury's finding she
appears only under her husband's name. The novel gives her back the days before May 18,
1927: a woman of fifty-one, short of breath, who notices everything on her own farm and
concludes almost nothing, because the alternative is a sentence no woman in that township
could have said aloud and been believed. Set against her days, in short chapters, is the
coroner's inquest that followed. It is a quiet, unsettling book about who a record leaves
out, and it is available on Amazon.
