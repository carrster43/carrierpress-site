# This week: the Jazz Age, written from inside

[Dancers in the Dark](/books/dancers-in-the-dark/) is the flapper novel written from the inside. Dorothy Speare was a twenty-four-year-old singer living her novel's central dilemma when it was published in 1922, the same season as Fitzgerald's Tales of the Jazz Age.

Her heroine, Joy Nelson, is twenty and arrives in a New England college town with a country stillness about her and a secret she barely trusts: she can sing. What she finds is a set of young people who cannot sit still, the Excitement-Eaters, who dance until the milk wagons roll and believe that the instant you stop moving, you lose your grip on life itself. Between a reliable young man and the pull of the stage, Joy has to decide whether a girl of the new age can keep love and her own gift, or must trade one for the other.

The same shelf holds another novel about a woman stepping out of the life she was handed. In [The Blue Castle](/books/the-blue-castle/), L. M. Montgomery's novel for adults from 1926, set in the pine-and-water wilderness of Ontario's Muskoka, Valancy Stirling is twenty-nine, has never once said what she thinks, and then decides to live.

# One to read

**[Dancers in the Dark](/books/dancers-in-the-dark/)** by Dorothy Speare. Its author went on to
Hollywood, where her story of a torn soprano became the film One Night of Love, but it all began here,
with a tired girl, a pair of silver slippers, and a dedication "To the Girls of Today." This edition
gives the complete 1922 novel with a preface placing it in its Jazz Age moment, an original prologue
and epilogue, and notes on the period slang. It is a candid, wistful portrait of youth chasing
stimulation that reads as recognizably a century on, and it is available on Amazon.
