Two weeks to Christmas, and the right time of year for a story read by lamplight. This
issue is about the man most people mean when they say "a ghost story at Christmas."

# This week: why ghosts belong to December

The habit of telling ghost stories at Christmas is older than M. R. James, but he is the
writer who fixed its shape. He was Provost of King's College, Cambridge, and one of the
great cataloguers of medieval manuscripts. The stories were a hobby. He wrote them to be read
aloud to friends by candlelight at Christmas, and most were first heard that way, at King's.

That is still the best way to meet them. They were written for one voice and a quiet room.
Nothing in them is loud. James threw out the castles and the picturesque past. He put an
ordinary modern scholar in a real place, gave him a document, and let something very old get
at him. His people are not undone by ignorance. They are undone by reading the reference
correctly.

He thought of all this as a pastime, and he reshaped the English ghost story so completely
that the form is now simply called Jamesian.

Start with [Ghost Stories of an Antiquary](/books/ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/), his first
collection, all eight stories in his order. The twelve that followed are in [More Ghost
Stories of an Antiquary](/books/more-ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/). If you want something
from the same shelf with no ghost in it at all, [The Willows](/books/the-willows/) is two
men on an island in the Danube, and a place that has noticed them.

# One to read

**[Ghost Stories of an Antiquary](/books/ghost-stories-of-an-antiquary/)** by M. R. James.
In his own note to the 1904 collection, James admits that nearly all of the learned
citations in these stories are invented, and this edition prints that note. Here are all
eight stories, in his order, from "Canon Alberic's Scrap-book" to "Oh, Whistle, and I'll
Come to You, My Lad", the one with the figure of crumpled linen in the neighbouring bed.
Each has a headnote, there is a glossary of the antiquarian world the plots turn on, and
Lovecraft's essay on James is reprinted at the back. "Who is this who is coming?" is a fair
question to ask on a December night, and the book is available on Amazon.
