# This week: the long game, at thirteen

Most lessons about money are about doing something. The hardest one is about waiting. A single
good habit, repeated for years, becomes a fortune, and almost nobody explains that to you
while you are young enough to use it.

[The Growing Fortune](/books/the-growing-fortune/) does. On the morning Alex turns thirteen,
the birthday gift is not a gift. It is a single sheet of paper: the paperwork for a custodial
investment account Alex's parents opened the day Alex was born, quietly growing for thirteen
years. Alex has never heard the words compound interest used about real money. What follows is
a coming-of-age story told in the language of money, with a first real mistake that costs more
than allowance, a rival whose shortcuts look smarter until they do not, and a market storm
that tests whether the lesson stuck.

The same idea sits underneath [Seeded for Success](/books/seeded-for-success/), written for
the grown-ups instead. Its opening point is that the most valuable thing you can give a child
is not money but time: decades for money to grow. One book tells the story, the other shows
the arithmetic year by year, in plain language with no hype.

# One to read

**[The Growing Fortune](/books/the-growing-fortune/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. It is a novel
about investing that teaches the real ideas, diversification, rebalancing, due diligence and
the long game, inside a story about growing up and deciding what kind of person you want to be
with money and without it. It is for young readers, for the parents who want to start the
conversation, and for anyone who wishes someone had explained all of this at thirteen. It
closes with a plain-language glossary. It is available on Amazon.
