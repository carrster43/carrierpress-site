# This week: the host who could not feed his guests

There is a poem in Oliver Herford's [Artful Anticks](/books/artful-anticks/) about an ant
with more social ambition than provisions. He invites five score guests to a ball, then sits
up all night working out how to feed them. Anyone who cooked for a crowd this week will know
exactly how he feels.

That is Herford's whole method: take a small creature, give it one entirely human ambition,
and follow that ambition with a perfectly straight face until it collapses. He wrote the
verse and drew every picture. It is light verse sharp enough for adults and charming enough
that children took it over, which makes it a fine book for a table where the youngest and the
oldest are both still sitting.

If your family's taste runs sharper, [The Laughing Willow](/books/the-laughing-willow/) is
Herford in 1918, and not the gentle book its title promises: adult satire, not a children's
book. For the youngest, [The Surprise Book](/books/the-surprise-book/) holds a story for
every month, collected by a girl named Dotty for a scrapbook that goes under the Christmas
tree.

# One to read

**[Artful Anticks](/books/artful-anticks/)** by Oliver Herford. Herford was called the
American Oscar Wilde for his talk, and this 1894 collection is the book that made the name
stick. In it a kitten sets sail across the Atlantic, because a cat may look at a king, so
surely a kitten may look at a prince. This edition restores all of Herford's pen-and-ink
drawings and adds an introduction on the vanished art of light verse. It is available on
Amazon.
