exactly what a cold weekend needs.

# This week: when the money has a reason

Most books that teach children about money start with the money: how to save it, how to
earn it, how it grows. The stories that stick start somewhere else, with a thing worth
saving.

In [Digital Gold Rush](/books/digital-gold-rush/), that thing is an eighty-year-old oak in
Chloe's backyard, with a treehouse in it that four kids have been climbing into since they
were eight. A hidden infection is working through the roots. The treatment costs eight
hundred dollars, it has to happen before the end of August, and between them they have
fifty-six.

So the money has a reason, and every skill in the book follows from it. Chloe opens a shop
for her art. Ben builds the creature game he has been designing in his head for months.
Alex and Maya start a tutoring service that teaches students to think rather than handing
them the answer. None of them has done any of it before, and the book does not pretend
otherwise: there is debugging at midnight and a quiet evening in the treehouse asking what
happens if they do not make it.

The same idea runs along the shelf. In [The Digital Founders](/books/the-digital-founders/),
a late-night annoyance becomes a company, and the hard parts turn out to be trust, a rival
and an ethical crossroads rather than the code.

# From the Labs

- **[Pantry](/apps/pantry/)** turns the meal planner around: it starts from what is already
  in the house and what goes off first, rather than from a recipe you have to shop for. Type
  one line, such as "chicken thighs fridge 3", or photograph a grocery receipt, and ask what
  you can make. Write what you cannot eat once, and every idea is told to keep it out,
  including as a hidden ingredient.

Every Carrier Labs app is on [the apps page](/apps/).

# One to read

**[Digital Gold Rush](/books/digital-gold-rush/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. The whole plan to
save a dying tree rests on four kids learning to code, to draw on a screen and to run a
business in one summer, starting from nothing. It is a story for readers ages 8 to 12 about
what it means to make something and put it into the world, including a game mechanic that
finally works after four hours of debugging and the moment of clicking publish and being too
scared to look. It is a good book to hand a young reader with a project in mind, and it is
available on Amazon.
