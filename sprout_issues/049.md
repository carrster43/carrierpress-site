# This week: the penny dreadful behind the legend

Everyone knows Sweeney Todd from the stage and the screen. Almost no one has read the book that created him.

It began as a penny dreadful, a romance sold in penny parts. The full 1850 penny-part version runs to more than one hundred and seventy chapters, which is why the complete, unabridged text comes in two volumes. [Sweeney Todd, Volume One](/books/sweeney-todd-volume-one/) carries chapters 1 to 84, opening on Fleet Street and following the tale to its darkest turn. [Sweeney Todd, Volume Two](/books/sweeney-todd-volume-two/) carries chapters 85 to 177, as the mystery of the string of pearls, the missing sailor and Mrs. Lovett's celebrated pies draws toward the light.

The edition adds an introduction on who wrote it, how the penny dreadful trade worked, and whether Todd was real, along with the original 1850 Preface and notes on the Fleet Street geography and Victorian slang.

The appetite it fed is older still. On the same shelf sits [The Monk](/books/the-monk/), published in 1796 when its author was just twenty: the Gothic novel so shocking that Parliament denounced it and Lewis was forced to censor it himself.

# One to read

**[Sweeney Todd, Volume One](/books/sweeney-todd-volume-one/)** by James Malcolm Rymer. A stranger walks
into a small barber's shop by St. Dunstan's church, and he is never seen to walk out. So begins the most
notorious penny dreadful ever written, with the murderous chair, the terrified apprentice Tobias and the
pie-maker Mrs. Lovett, reprinted here whole and unabridged. The edition says plainly that it
contains scenes of violence and menace consistent with the original text, which is rather the point. Read it the week before Halloween, by
lamplight if you can, and it is available on Amazon.
