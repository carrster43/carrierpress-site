# This week: when the paperwork is too tidy

Old records rarely lie with forgeries. They lie with neatness. A column that adds up too well, a list in an order nobody questions, a name that appears exactly where it should. The novels of Things Left Open are built on that idea. Each one hands an heir of the Greek diaspora a document that ought to be dull, and each time the dullness is the disguise.

In [The Pearl Ledger](/books/the-pearl-ledger/), Eleni Argyros is a maritime lawyer in Sydney who reads old ship registries the way other people read weather. Her brief is to clear a 1907 Greek pearling registry so a coastal consortium can build along the Dampier Peninsula. It looks like routine work until two of the four vessel names turn out to be the names that broke her late father's career, in a case he was never allowed to explain.

The shelf opens with [The Sponge Cache](/books/the-sponge-cache/), where a grandmother leaves Marina a ledger that does not balance, and a stranger is already willing to pay far too much for it. In [The Sixth Cache](/books/the-sixth-cache/), the record is not on paper at all. It is a song, and a researcher listening to his grandmother sing it for the ninth time counts the saints in her verse and finds they fall in an order no Orthodox calendar has ever kept.

Each novel stands alone. Read in order, they become one long accounting of who was owed, and who was expected to forget.

# One to read

**[The Pearl Ledger](/books/the-pearl-ledger/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. The order of four ship names in
a 1907 registry is not an order at all: it is a set of coordinates, pointing to a strongbox at Cygnet
Bay. Book 2 of Things Left Open is literary suspense about a century of debts and deaths across every
community that worked the pearl beds, and about a lawyer who can use the ledger or walk away, but
cannot do both cleanly. It is a fine late summer read for anyone who likes a mystery that turns on
what a document leaves out, and it is available on Amazon.
