# This week: a novel about looking

The end of the year is when people take stock, which makes it a good week for a book about
what it costs to see clearly. [The Fortune Weaver](/books/the-fortune-weaver/) is an austere
literary novel set inside a finance thriller. Alex Vance lost everything in 2021 and is
rebuilding a quiet practice from a one-bedroom apartment in Inwood, with a handful of clients
and one rule for every analyst he hires: find your second trade. Then Elias Thorne calls with
an opportunity. The numbers work. The structure is, if not legal, then in a gray space nobody
has looked at yet. Alex says yes.

The other books on the [Standalone Novels](/fiction/#h-standalone) shelf share that moment,
when somebody decides whether to look away. In [The Last Adviser](/books/the-last-adviser/),
Adam Halsey has forty years of a Boston wealth firm behind him, six months to hand it on,
and a small book of old accounts that the next person will be tempted to dismantle, and must not.

# From the Labs

- **[Who Serves](/apps/whoserves/)** puts everyone who governs your address on one screen:
  the districts, the offices, the names, and when each is next up for election. Every name
  shows where it was read and when it was last checked, and when a check is too old, the app
  says so rather than vouching for the name.

# One to read

**[The Fortune Weaver](/books/the-fortune-weaver/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. Alex Vance lost
everything in 2021, and the novel opens as he is offered the chance to lose more. His analyst
understands what he has agreed to before he does, and so, eventually, does the lawyer of
thirty-six years who lays out the four paths still open to him. It is written in deliberately
spare prose, and the voice is the reason to read it on a quiet night. It is available on
Amazon.
