# This week: a mythology, assembled in order

Robert W. Chambers did not invent Carcosa, Hali or Hastur. He borrowed all three names from Ambrose Bierce.

In 1886 Bierce wrote a short story about a man lost in a ruined country, trying to find his way back to a city called Carcosa. Five years later he gave a gentle god named Hastur to a shepherd. Then in 1895 Chambers took the names, changed what they meant, and built the most influential nightmare in American weird fiction. Lovecraft read both of them, and said so.

[Carcosa](/books/carcosa/) prints those pieces in the order they were written: Bierce's "An Inhabitant of Carcosa" and "Haita the Shepherd", then the four Yellow Sign stories from Chambers, so you can watch a mythology being assembled. Every piece has a dated headnote, the glossary flags every name that began with Bierce, and an appendix from Lovecraft's Supernatural Horror in Literature shows him stating the debt plainly. It runs about 130 pages, short enough for a weekend.

Read it before or after [The King in Yellow](/books/the-king-in-yellow/), the 1895 book in which Chambers never once lets you read the cursed play at its centre; you only ever meet the people it has already ruined. Then try [The Mystery of Choice](/books/the-mystery-of-choice/), which Chambers published two years later and which Lovecraft never mentioned.

# One to read

**[Carcosa](/books/carcosa/)** by Ambrose Bierce and Robert W. Chambers. Hastur, a name weird fiction
later made infamous, began in Bierce as a gentle god given to a shepherd. This annotated
edition sets the Bierce stories beside the Chambers tales that borrowed from them, with an original
introduction tracing the line from Bierce to Chambers to Lovecraft and beyond. It is the right book for
the first week of October, short and strange, and it is available on Amazon.
