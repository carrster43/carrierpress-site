# This week: puzzles for the longest nights

There is a reason the locked door and the hidden clue feel right in winter. A puzzle mystery
asks you to sit still, pay attention and keep track, which is easier with the curtains drawn.
And the best ones set the puzzle inside a house: rooms nobody has counted, doors that are
locked for a reason, letters that say one thing and mean another.

[The Billionaire's Legacy Game](/books/the-billionaire-s-legacy-game/) builds exactly that
house. Seventeen-year-old Al has an ordinary life of calculus quizzes, a part-time job and a
dad lost in the financial pages, until a summons arrives from the estate of a billionaire Al
has never met. It is not a condolence card. It is the opening move of a game. To claim a
legacy that should not exist, Al has to enter the Thorne estate and solve a chain of puzzles
left behind by a man who trusted no one: a library built like a labyrinth, a vault sealed by
an enigma, coded letters, and a blackmail document that proves the family's polished surface
is a lie.

It sits on the Money Skills and Making Things shelf beside other novels where a teenager meets
real money for the first time. In [The Growing Fortune](/books/the-growing-fortune/), it
arrives on a thirteenth birthday as a single sheet of paper. The puzzle there is slower, and
it takes years to solve.

# One to read

**[The Billionaire's Legacy Game](/books/the-billionaire-s-legacy-game/)** by Jeffrey L.
Carrier. The Thorne estate has more rooms than anyone has counted, and somewhere among the
relatives, lawyers and staff who smile too easily is someone willing to do anything to stop Al
reaching the end of the game. It is a twisting inheritance mystery for readers who love
locked-room clues and a hero in far over their head who refuses to fold, and the real puzzle
is not the fortune but who can be trusted to help find it. A long book for long evenings, and
it is available on Amazon.
