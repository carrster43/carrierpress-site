# This week: why a merchant's house is full of paper

The Keystone Cycle puts five Greek diaspora teenagers in a converted sponge merchant's house in
Pothia, on Kalymnos, for two years of a four-year exchange programme. After that, each of them
goes on to the place their own family left: Tarpon Springs, Darwin, Sfax, Alexandria, Broome.

The house has five bedrooms, a common room with a piano under a tarp, and a storage room left
alone since the house stopped being a business. That room is where the plots start, and there
is a real reason it would be full. A sponge merchant was a banker whether he wanted to be or
not. Boats went out on money that did not exist yet, against a catch nobody had seen, and the
merchant who fronted it kept the account. When the trade ended, the boats were sold and the
paper stayed, because paper is not worth carrying out.

So each lead turns up a document first. In [The Drosos Ledger](/books/the-drosos-ledger/) it
is a page in a back-room museum ledger. In [The Hudson Creek Record](/books/the-hudson-creek-record/)
it is a parish marriage record from 1908. In [The Sfax Letters](/books/the-sfax-letters/) it is
a stack of letters labelled in Sfax-Greek, the top one from 1903.

None of these documents is a villain. Each one is simply a second version of a family story
that everyone thought had only one.

# One to read

**[The Hudson Creek Record](/books/the-hudson-creek-record/)** by Jeffrey L. Carrier. In a
storage room one January night, Niko Lekkas finds a parish marriage record from 1908: the name on
it is his, and the town on it is not. His family's story had been edited down to a single
sentence for a Darwin school principal in 1958. Two years later, back in Darwin, he apprentices
at a Larrakia family's boat-building yard on Hudson Creek, learns to lay a keel and learns the
tide-names. It is a quiet coming-of-age novel about how a grandmother's version can be true and
still not be the only version, and it is available on Amazon.
