# This week: when the future was a guess

Some of the most interesting books on the Classics Line were written by people working at
the edge of what their century knew. Read now, they are not useful as forecasts. They are
useful as a record of how a mind reaches past its evidence, and of what it was like to stand
where they stood.

[A Honeymoon in Space](/books/a-honeymoon-in-space/) sends newlyweds on a tour of the solar
system in a private spaceship, a vision of space travel written two years before the Wright
brothers left the ground. The edition's introduction looks at Griffith and at what 1901
imagined space travel would be.

[6,000 Tons of Gold](/books/6000-tons-of-gold/) asks what would happen if one man suddenly
owned more gold than most governments on earth. It appeared in 1894, a year after the Panic
of 1893, while America tore itself apart over the gold standard, and it is an adventure, an
early scientific romance and a financial satire at once.

And [The Animate and the Inanimate](/books/the-animate-and-the-inanimate/) is the strangest
of the three: a 1925 argument that almost every law of physics runs the same backward as
forward, except one, and that the half of the universe we call alive is the half where that
one runs in reverse. The edition
is careful about it. Its introduction includes a section on what the book is not a
prediction of, and a headnote before every chapter marks where Sidis is right, where he is
wrong, and where the physics of 1925 simply did not have the tools.

That last kind of note is what makes an old speculative book worth having: not the claim,
but an honest map of it.

# One to read

**[The Animate and the Inanimate](/books/the-animate-and-the-inanimate/)** by William James
Sidis. In January 1910 a boy of eleven stood in front of about a hundred Harvard professors
and talked for two hours about four dimensional bodies, and in 1925, at twenty seven, he
published this book under his own name. Its idea is simple to state and hard to shake:
reverse the second law of thermodynamics and heat gathers instead of spreading, and Sidis
follows that reversal wherever it leads, all the way to the claim on the cover: the half
running backward is the half we call alive. This annotated edition sets
the famous IQ figure aside in a paragraph, adds a glossary of the private vocabulary he built
for the argument, and prints the 1940 privacy case a magazine profile forced on him, so the
man and the argument can both be read on their own terms. It is available on Amazon.
