# This week: a name passed from hand to hand

Some of the best known places in horror were borrowed. Carcosa is one of them.

In 1886 Ambrose Bierce wrote a very short story about a man lost in a ruined country,
trying to find his way back to a city called Carcosa. A few years later he gave a gentle god
named Hastur to a shepherd. Then, in 1895, Robert W. Chambers took both names for The King
in Yellow, changed what they meant, and built a play that ruins whoever reads it. Lovecraft
read them both and built on what he found.

So the trail runs Bierce, then Chambers, then Lovecraft, and each writer took a word and
left something different behind it. That is the pleasure of reading them side by side: you
can watch a mythology being put together, one borrowing at a time.

[Carcosa](/books/carcosa/) prints the pieces in the order they were written, the two Bierce
stories beside the four Yellow Sign tales, so the borrowing reads as a sequence rather than
a rumour. It runs about 130 pages, short enough for a weekend.

If you would rather start at the source, [Can Such Things Be?](/books/can-such-things-be/)
is Bierce's own collection, the book where he placed the Carcosa story himself. And if you
want to see what Chambers did with it, [The King in Yellow](/books/the-king-in-yellow/) is
where the Yellow Sign comes from.

# One to read

**[Can Such Things Be?](/books/can-such-things-be/)** by Ambrose Bierce. The Carcosa story
was not in the first edition of this book in 1893. Bierce moved it there at the end of his
life, together with the story that names Hastur, when he rearranged his fiction for his
Collected Works, and that later book is the one Lovecraft read. This edition prints those
twenty-four stories in his order, with a headnote before each one, notes on the real and
invented places, and a comparison of the printings that shows what he changed, down to wolves
becoming bears. It is the right book for a dark December evening and the place where a whole
mythology quietly starts, and it is available on Amazon.
