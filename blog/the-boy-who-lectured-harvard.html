A gift for the reader who has everything: the boy who lectured Harvard at eleven

Every list has one. The reader who has read the popular science shelf twice, who already owns the big book about time, and who will politely thank you for the next one. This is a book they have almost certainly never seen.

In January 1910 a boy of eleven stood in front of about a hundred Harvard professors and talked for two hours about four dimensional bodies. He had entered the college that autumn as its youngest student. The newspapers took him and never let go, and William James Sidis spent most of the thirty years left to him trying to get out from under it. He worked as a clerk. He ran adding machines. He published under invented names.

In 1925, at twenty seven, he published one book under his own: The Animate and the Inanimate.

The question at the heart of it

Almost every law of physics runs the same backward as forward. One does not. Heat spreads, order runs down, and the cup never reassembles itself on the table. Sidis asked what life looks like against that law, since a living thing gathers energy and builds order instead of losing it.

He follows the reversal all the way out. If the law can run backward somewhere, what does a universe look like where it does? The road takes him to a Great Collision, and then to Great Explosions: a universe beginning in one hot, dense event. He does not believe it. He raises it to show that the law which led him there cannot be the whole story. Georges Lemaitre proposed the same shape of beginning, in earnest, six years later.

That is the pleasure of the book. It is a young mind, alone, with no university behind it, pushing a single idea until it breaks, and writing down honestly where it went.

What makes this edition the one to give

The 1925 text is short and strange, and most readers need a guide beside them. This edition gives them one without getting in the way:

  • The complete text, all nineteen chapters and Sidis's own preface, set from Harvard College

Library's copy of the first edition.

  • A new introduction, including a section on what this book is not a prediction of, so

the reader meets the argument rather than the legend around it.

  • A headnote before every chapter, marking where he is right, where he is wrong, and where

the physics of 1925 simply did not have the tools.

  • A glossary of the private vocabulary he built for the argument, and the three original

figures.

  • The 1940 court case a magazine profile forced on him, printed entire. It is still read in

privacy law, and it is the last word on how the world treated him.

The famous IQ figure gets one paragraph and is set aside. No test anyone has produced supports it, and it is the least interesting thing about him.

Who to give it to

The engineer who reads history. The teenager who is bored in physics class because it is too easy. The grandparent who still argues about entropy at dinner. Anyone who has ever been called gifted and wished people would stop.
