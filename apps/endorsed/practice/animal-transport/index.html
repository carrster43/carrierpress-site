Animal Transport Practice Questions

There is no federal license or exam for livestock haulers or horse transporters. What the federal rules require is registration, paperwork and care standards: a carrier (a business transporting animals for hire) or an intermediate handler of animals covered by the Animal Welfare Act (such as dogs, cats, and animals used for research, exhibition or as pets) registers with USDA APHIS and files a new form every 3 years; an owner/shipper hauling horses to slaughter fills in and signs an owner-shipper certificate for each animal; and interstate livestock travels with the health and ID papers in 9 CFR 86. A CDL is needed only when the vehicle itself requires one. States, buyers and packers may add their own training or permits, such as an industry livestock handling course; ask them which apply to you.

These are the first 10 of the 40 questions in Endorsed's Animal Transport pack, with the first of its 10 study sections. Every answer names the rule it comes from. The full pack, and 18 other credentials, are in Endorsed.

10 practice questions

  1. Under the Twenty-Eight Hour Law, animals may be confined in a vehicle without unloading for feed, water and rest for no more than:

    1. 24 consecutive hours
    2. 28 consecutive hours
    3. 36 consecutive hours, always
    4. 48 consecutive hours
    Show the answer

    B. 28 consecutive hours

    The statute sets 28 consecutive hours, unless one of its exceptions applies (sheep at night, unavoidable causes, or 36 hours on the owner's written request).

    Source: 49 U.S.C. 80502(a)

  2. How can the 28 hour period be extended to 36 hours?

    1. The driver takes a 10 hour off duty break on the way
    2. The owner asks in writing, apart from the bill of lading
    3. The trip stays under 500 miles from origin to destination
    Show the answer

    B. The owner asks in writing, apart from the bill of lading

    The owner or person having custody must ask in writing, separate from the bill of lading or other rail form.

    Source: 49 U.S.C. 80502(a)

  3. Under Part 89, livestock delayed en route so their confinement materially exceeds the Twenty-Eight Hour Law's limit should get:

    1. Extra feed in proportion to the excess time
    2. No feed until they reach the destination
    3. Water only, with no extra feed at all
    Show the answer

    A. Extra feed in proportion to the excess time

    Part 89 says that when a delay makes confinement materially exceed the law's period, the livestock should get additional feed in proportion to the excess time.

    Source: 9 CFR 89.1(d)

  4. Livestock held at a feeding station 12 hours after their last feed was substantially eaten should be fed again, unless the hold plus the time to the next station or destination would not ordinarily exceed:

    1. 28 hours
    2. 40 hours
    3. 36 hours
    4. 24 hours
    Show the answer

    B. 40 hours

    Part 89 lets them be held longer without that feeding if the hold plus the time to the next feeding station or destination, whichever is closer, would not ordinarily exceed 40 hours.

    Source: 9 CFR 89.2

  5. Part 89 says livestock should be unloaded into pens for feed, water and rest unless:

    1. The load is under 20 head
    2. The weather on the route stays mild
    3. All can lie down at once in the car
    4. The driver is still within hours
    Show the answer

    C. All can lie down at once in the car

    Unloading is called for unless there is ample room in the car for all of the animals to lie down at the same time.

    Source: 9 CFR 89.3(a)

  6. When hogs are watered in the vehicle, water should be available for not less than:

    1. 1 hour
    2. 15 minutes
    3. 5 hours
    Show the answer

    A. 1 hour

    In the case of hogs, water should be available for not less than 1 hour.

    Source: 9 CFR 89.3(b)

  7. Can equines for slaughter ride in a trailer with two stacked decks?

    1. Yes, if each deck has enough headroom
    2. No, they ride on one level only
    3. Yes, for ponies and donkeys only
    Show the answer

    B. No, they ride on one level only

    Equines for slaughter may not ride in a cargo space with two or more stacked levels; the 2006 allowance has ended and floating decks must be set to one level.

    Source: 9 CFR 88.3(b)

  8. Under Part 88, who is an owner/shipper?

    1. Anyone who hauls a single horse to a sale barn
    2. The slaughter plant that buys the horses
    3. One who hauls over 20 a year to slaughter
    Show the answer

    C. One who hauls over 20 a year to slaughter

    An owner/shipper commercially transports more than 20 equines a year to slaughter, unless it is incidental to producing food or fiber.

    Source: 9 CFR 88.1

  9. On a slaughter load, each stallion and each aggressive equine must be:

    1. Completely segregated from the others
    2. Tied short at the front of the trailer
    3. Loaded last, nearest the rear door
    Show the answer

    A. Completely segregated from the others

    Each stallion and any aggressive equine is completely segregated so it cannot contact any other equine.

    Source: 9 CFR 88.4(a)(4)(ii)

  10. Before loading for slaughter, each equine gets food, water and rest for at least:

    1. 2 consecutive hours
    2. 12 consecutive hours
    3. 6 consecutive hours
    Show the answer

    C. 6 consecutive hours

    The owner/shipper provides food, potable water and rest for not less than 6 consecutive hours immediately before loading.

    Source: 9 CFR 88.4(a)(1)

Study: Which federal rules reach an animal hauler

This pack is study preparation, not an exam. No federal agency tests animal haulers; the rules below are what USDA and FMCSA expect you to follow on the road.

Livestock in long interstate trips: the Twenty-Eight Hour Law (49 U.S.C. 80502), with USDA's statement of policy under it in 9 CFR 89. Horses and other equines hauled to slaughter: 9 CFR 88. Health papers and ID for cattle, bison, horses, poultry, sheep, goats, swine and captive cervids crossing a state line: 9 CFR 86. Moving sick animals and cleaning trucks: 9 CFR 71.

Dogs, cats, primates, rabbits and other warmblooded animals used or meant for research, teaching, testing, experimentation, exhibition or as pets: the Animal Welfare Act rules in 9 CFR 1, 2 and 3. Under 9 CFR 1.1, "animal" for those rules excludes horses not used for research and farm animals such as livestock or poultry used for food or fiber, so a cattle or hog hauler is not an AWA carrier for those loads.

An AWA carrier is the operator of an airline, railroad, motor carrier, shipping line or other enterprise in the business of transporting any animals for hire. An intermediate handler is a business that receives custody of animals in connection with their transport in commerce and is not a dealer, research facility, exhibitor, auction operator or carrier. A pet owner driving their own dog is neither: the carrier definition needs a business of transporting animals for hire, and the intermediate handler definition a business that takes custody of animals for their transport.

Hours of service: FMCSA's livestock exemption is in 49 CFR 395.1(k). The CDL itself has no livestock endorsement; whether you need a CDL depends on the vehicle, as in the CDL General Knowledge pack. States may add rules of their own on top of all of these; check yours.

For dogs, the 9 CFR 1.1 definition of "animal" means all dogs, including those used for hunting, security or breeding, so a business hauling working dogs for hire is an AWA carrier just as it is for pets. The same section counts horses and llamas used solely as work and pack animals as farm animals, and "animal" excludes horses not used for research and other farm animals.

Source: 9 CFR 1.1; 9 CFR 71.3; 9 CFR 86.2; 9 CFR 88.1; 9 CFR 89.1; 49 U.S.C. 80502; 49 CFR 395.1(k)
