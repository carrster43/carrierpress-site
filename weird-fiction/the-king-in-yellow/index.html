Weird fiction

The King in Yellow, explained

The King in Yellow is a book of ten stories by Robert W. Chambers, published in 1895. The first four turn around a play, also called The King in Yellow, whose second act drives its readers mad. Chambers never lets you read it.

Along the shore the cloud waves break, The twin suns sink behind the lake, The shadows lengthen In Carcosa.

A book about a play you never get to read

Inside the stories, The King in Yellow is a play in two acts. The first act is harmless, even lovely. Anyone who reads on into the second act is ruined: driven mad, damned, or changed past recovery. The play is banned and burned across Europe, and it keeps turning up anyway, and every character who finds a copy reads it.

Chambers quotes only a handful of lines: Cassilda's song, a few stage directions, and one exchange at a masked ball, where Camilla asks a stranger to unmask. He answers that he wears no mask, and she whispers in terror, “No mask? No mask!” The rest is a hole in the middle of the book, and the reader fills it.

That restraint is the whole trick. Because you never read the play, it can never disappoint you. Every reader writes their own second act.

Who wrote it

Robert W. Chambers (1865 to 1933) trained as a painter, at the Art Students League in New York and then at the Académie Julian in Paris, and showed at the Paris Salon before he published a word. The Latin Quarter of the late 1880s is all through the book: half its stories follow American art students in Paris.

The King in Yellow was his second book, and it holds nearly all the weird fiction he ever wrote. He went on to become one of the best paid popular novelists in America, writing romances that are now forgotten. H. P. Lovecraft ranked the best of these stories among the finest horror in the language, and mourned that Chambers abandoned the vein.

Where it went next

Lovecraft took up the names: Hastur, the Lake of Hali and the Yellow Sign surface in “The Whisperer in Darkness,” and his circle wove them into what became the Cthulhu Mythos. Raymond Chandler borrowed the title for a story of his own. Role-playing games, comics and bands kept the Sign in circulation, and in 2014 the first season of True Detective sent a new generation looking for Carcosa and the Yellow King.
