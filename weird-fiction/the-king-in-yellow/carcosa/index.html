The King in Yellow

Carcosa, Hastur and Hali: where the names come from

Chambers did not invent Carcosa. He took the name from Ambrose Bierce's 1886 story “An Inhabitant of Carcosa,” where it is a ruin. He took Hali from the same story, where Hali is a sage, and Hastur from Bierce's “Haïta the Shepherd” (1891), where Hastur is a kindly god of shepherds.

Bierce, 1886: Carcosa is a ruin

“An Inhabitant of Carcosa” is a very short story about a man walking through a desolate country, trying to find his way back to the city of Carcosa, and slowly understanding what has happened to him. In Bierce, Carcosa is an ancient city fallen so long ago that the forest has eaten it. Nothing lives there. The story's epigraph is credited to Hali, a sage: a voice, not a place.

Bierce, 1891: Hastur is kind

“Haïta the Shepherd” is a fable about a young shepherd visited by Happiness, who loses her each time he tries to hold her or ask her to explain herself. Haïta prays to Hastur, and Bierce's Hastur is a benign god who looks after flocks and simple men. A reader who expects the Hastur of later horror is usually startled by how gentle he is.

Chambers, 1895: the names turned inside out

Chambers took the names and none of their meanings. Carcosa stopped being a ruin and became a destination, where the King reigns under black stars. Hali stopped being a sage and became the lake beside it. Hastur appears in a list of dynastic ravings with no hint whether it is a person, a place or a title. Then Chambers added the thing that was all his own: the play.

Lovecraft and Derleth: Hastur gets a biography

Lovecraft read Chambers in 1927 and dropped Hastur into “The Whisperer in Darkness” as one name in a list, beside Yuggoth and the Lake of Hali. After Lovecraft's death, August Derleth tidied the Mythos and made Hastur a Great Old One, a half brother to Cthulhu, with a place in a hierarchy. None of that is in Lovecraft, still less in Chambers, and it is the opposite of Bierce.

So there are three Hasturs: Bierce's kindly god, Chambers's word that will not hold still, and Derleth's monster. Most readers meet the last one first.

True Detective and The King in Yellow

In 2014 the first season of True Detective, written by Nic Pizzolatto, put Carcosa and the Yellow King into the Louisiana bayou and never explained the reference. It did to Chambers what Chambers did to Bierce: took the shine, left the old meaning behind, and built something new in the gap. Viewers went looking, and found their way back to a book from 1895.

Carcosa as a place in Louisiana is the show's own. In Chambers it is a city on a lake under twin suns, which is stranger.
