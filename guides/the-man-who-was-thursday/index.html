The Man Who Was Thursday: discussion questions

10 questions for book clubs, classrooms and solo readers, with a G. K. Chesterton timeline, from the Reader's Guide in the Carrier Press annotated edition of The Man Who Was Thursday by G. K. Chesterton.

This guide is printed at the back of the Carrier Press annotated edition.

Questions for reflection and discussion

These questions avoid spoiling the ending; a spoiler-walled Afterword follows for readers who want to go further.

  1. Chesterton subtitles the book A Nightmare. How early did you sense that you were reading something other than a realistic thriller, and which moments felt most dreamlike to you?
  2. The novel imagines a police force that fights ideas rather than crimes, on the theory that a dangerous philosophy is more destructive than a dangerous man. Do you find that idea persuasive, chilling, absurd, or all three?
  3. Gabriel Syme is a "poet of order" who defends lamp-posts and timetables against people who find them boring. What is Chesterton getting at by making respectability and routine into things worth defending, even romantic?
  4. Each member of the Council wears the mask of a day of the week, and each carries a particular kind of fear. Which of the days unsettled you most, and why do you think Chesterton gives dread so many different faces?
  5. The book is genuinely funny even at its most frightening. How does Chesterton's humor change the way the menace lands? Would the story work without it?
  6. Syme spends much of the novel feeling utterly alone against an overwhelming conspiracy. How does the theme of secret, unsuspected allies shape the story, and did it change how you read the earlier chapters once you knew more?
  7. The dedication poem remembers the pessimism of the 1890s, a time when "science announced nonentity and art admired decay." How does that historical despair show up inside the novel, and how does the book fight it?
  8. London itself, its fog, its restaurants, its river, is described with loving solidity even as the plot grows fantastic. What does that grounding in a real, ordinary city do for a story that keeps threatening to fly apart?
  9. Chesterton loved paradox, the idea that a truth and its opposite can both be real. Where did you notice the novel turning things into their opposites, and did those reversals feel like cheating or like revelation?
  10. When you reached the final chapter, what was your honest first reaction: exhilaration, confusion, awe, frustration? What do you think the book is finally about? (The Afterword takes up this question in detail.)

G. K. Chesterton: a brief timeline

1874
Born Gilbert Keith Chesterton in London.
1890s
Studies art at the Slade School and literature at University College London; passes through a period of intense pessimism and near-breakdown that shapes his later thought.
1900
Publishes his first books of verse; begins a prolific career as a journalist and essayist.
1904
Publishes his first novel, The Napoleon of Notting Hill.
1908
Publishes Orthodoxy, his classic account of his own return to Christian belief, and The Man Who Was Thursday: A Nightmare.
1911
Publishes The Innocence of Father Brown, introducing the priest-detective who became his most famous character.
1922
Received into the Roman Catholic Church, a step his writing had long been moving toward.
1936
Dies at Beaconsfield, England, having written some eighty books, hundreds of poems, and thousands of essays. His admirers ranged from Jorge Luis Borges to Kingsley Amis, and The Man Who Was Thursday remained the novel readers argued about most.

If you loved The Man Who Was Thursday

Try Chesterton's Father Brown mysteries, beginning with The Innocence of Father Brown (1911), for the same delight in paradox on a smaller, sharper scale, and his first novel, The Napoleon of Notting Hill (1904), for more of his romance of ordinary things. Readers drawn to the book's dreamlike reversals and its arguments with despair might look next at the fiction of Jorge Luis Borges and Franz Kafka, both of whom admired it.
